# Scenarios

Fifteen short narratives, grouped by persona, each opening with the objective it
serves. The people are the five in `personas/personas-claude.md`, so their skills,
patience, and motivation carry over rather than being restated here. Where the
current paper-and-shouting setup cannot solve the problem, the narrative says so;
where the product as currently scoped cannot either, it says that too.

---

## Marisol Ruiz — owner-operator

### 1. Changing the menu on a Monday afternoon

**Objective:** keep the menu the guests see accurate, without help from anyone
else.

The fish supplier substituted hake for the cod, and the cod dish has to come off
and a hake dish go on at a slightly higher price. Marisol does this from the back
office between counting invoices, in the fifteen minutes before she collects her
son. She finds the cod, pulls it, adds the hake under the same category, types the
description and the new price, and that is the end of it. What used to follow this
was the part she hated: the printed menus were now wrong and the QR menu her nephew
built was already nine months stale, so guests would order a dish that no longer
existed and a server would have to walk it back at the table. The old setup had no
way for her to change the menu herself and have guests see it the same night.

### 2. Deciding at the end of the first month whether to keep it

**Objective:** work out whether the thing she is paying for actually improved
service.

It is the last Monday of the trial and Marisol is trying to answer one question.
Her impression is good — the floor is calmer, she has not apologised for a wrong
plate in two weeks — but her impression is all she has. She goes looking for a
number, something like remakes this month against remakes before, and there is
nothing to find: the product records what was ordered and what it cost, but
reports nothing back. She ends up deciding on feel and on what Devin and Tom tell
her, which is how she decides most things but not how she decides about money.
**This is a gap in the product as scoped, not in the old setup** — paper gave her
no numbers either, but paper was free.

---

### 3. Setting up a new server before his first shift

**Objective:** give a new hire everything he needs to take orders on Friday, and
nothing he does not.

Eli starts tomorrow, so on Thursday afternoon Marisol creates him a user and gives
it the Server role. That lets him open orders, add items, mark things served, split
a check and settle it, and it deliberately does not let him change a price or edit
the menu, which is hers alone. It takes her about a minute, and she does the same
thing every few months because the floor turns over. The old setup had no
equivalent step at all — a new server was handed a pad, which meant there was
nothing to set up and equally nothing stopping them from doing anything. What she
still cannot do is give him somewhere to practise before a live table, because
there is no training mode.

## Devin Okafor — server

### 4. Taking an order with real special requests

**Objective:** get four covers' worth of detail to the kitchen without anything
being misread.

Table six, four people, and three of them want something changed: one allergy, one
steak temperature, one plate to be split in the kitchen for two guests to share.
Devin takes it on the handheld while standing at the table, typing each note onto
the item it belongs to, and sends it without walking anywhere. On paper this was
the moment things went wrong. He would abbreviate under pressure, Tom would read
his handwriting across a hot line, and if the allergy note was ambiguous the plate
came back — which cost the restaurant a portion and cost Devin his tip. Handwriting
is the part the old setup could never fix.

### 5. Finding out that food is ready

**Objective:** get plates to the table while they are still hot.

Devin is three tables away, taking a drinks order, when Tom taps table six's mains
to Ready. Nothing reaches him: the handheld is asleep in his apron, notifications
are in-app only, and the alert is waiting on a screen he is not looking at. What
actually gets him moving is Tom calling out from the pass, exactly as it did
before. When Devin next glances at the handheld — a minute later, maybe three — the
badge is there and he goes. **The product improves on the old setup here only
partly:** he can see item status without walking to the pass, but he still learns
that food is up by being told by a human. This is the largest single gap between
what he wanted and what v1 delivers.

### 6. Guests move, and two tables become one

**Objective:** keep an order attached to the right table when the room changes
shape.

A two-top at table four is halfway through drinks when two friends arrive, so
Devin moves them to table eleven, which seats four. He moves their order across to
eleven, and their items keep the statuses they already had. Later the same evening
a different pair of tables pushed together to seat a party of six have two open
orders between them, so he merges them into one so the check settles as a single
bill. The kitchen's queue is unaffected by either action; the items are the same
items, and Tom never needs to know the room was rearranged. On paper the ticket
said table four for the rest of the night, and the way that got fixed was somebody
remembering to say so out loud, which is how a runner ends up putting a plate in
front of the wrong guests.

### 7. Handing over tables at the end of a shift

**Objective:** transfer responsibility for open tables to the server taking over.

Devin's shift ends at nine and two of his tables are mid-meal. He hands both orders
to the server who is picking up his section, and the transfer is recorded, so the
orders now show as hers. She sees them in her own list, she gets the alerts when
their items go Ready, and any question about table seven now goes to her. Devin
leaves without anything half-owned behind him. Under the old setup this was a
verbal "table seven's yours" on the way out the door, which worked until it was
forgotten — and then a plate came up for a table nobody was watching.

### 8. Pulling a dish the kitchen has just run out of

**Objective:** stop the rest of the floor selling something he has just been told
is gone.

Devin picks up two plates at the pass and Tom tells him that was the last of the
hake. Rather than carrying that fact around in his head, Devin marks the hake
unavailable from his handheld on the walk back to the floor. It comes off the guest
menu on every phone in the room and can no longer be added to an order, including
by Eli, who has no idea the conversation happened. Either role can do this, which
matters because the information reaches whoever happens to be standing at the pass,
not whoever has the authority. Under the old setup this was Devin telling the other
servers one at a time as he passed them, and someone selling the hake anyway
because they were at a table when he walked by.

### 9. Splitting a six-top who shared a bottle

**Objective:** settle a complicated check quickly enough that no queue forms
behind him.

Six guests, and they want to pay in four ways: two couples paying together, two
people paying alone, and a bottle of wine and a shared platter that everyone had
some of. Devin makes four parts and assigns each guest's own dishes to their part.
He then divides the bottle and the platter across all four, which splits each into
four even shares; where a share does not divide cleanly it is rounded up to the
cent, so all four parts carry the same amount for that item and the four parts
together come to a few cents more than the order total. One couple pays at the
register and their part locks, leaving three parts open. One of the remaining guests
then offers to cover their friend's dishes, so Devin moves those items onto that
part and re-divides the shares across the three parts that are still unpaid; the
settled part is untouched. On paper this was arithmetic done at speed on the back of
a ticket, with a queue building behind him and a real chance of the totals not
adding up.

## Tom Baran — kitchen lead

### 10. Working the queue through a Saturday rush

**Objective:** cook everything in the right order without being interrupted.

It is 8:15 and the TV across the kitchen is full. Tom reads the queue from where he
stands, calls the next items, and taps each one to In progress and then to Ready on
the tablet at the pass as it goes out. What he notices most is what is missing:
nobody has walked up to ask him about a table all evening, because the servers can
see item status for themselves. On the old rail he lost focus four or five times a
service to that question alone. What the rail did better, and he feels this, is let
him slide a ticket left when a table's timing changed — **the digital queue cannot
be reordered, and this is a live gap in the design, not a solved problem.**

### 11. Running out of the salmon

**Objective:** stop the salmon being sold the moment the last portion is plated.

The last salmon goes on a plate at 8:40. Tom marks it unavailable on the pass
tablet, and that is the whole task: it disappears from the guest menu on every
phone in the room and cannot be added to a new order. Under the old setup he
shouted "86 the salmon" at whoever was closest and hoped it travelled. It usually
did not travel fast enough, and three more tickets for salmon would arrive before
the floor caught up, each one a table that had to be told no after they had ordered.

### 12. A cancelled item he has already started

**Objective:** stop cooking something nobody is going to eat.

Table nine changes their mind about the lamb two minutes after ordering it, and
Devin cancels it from the floor. The lamb is already on Tom's line. Instead of the
item quietly vanishing from a board he has memorised, he is told explicitly that a
started item was cancelled, so he pulls it and knows why. On paper the ticket got
removed from the rail by whoever remembered, or did not get removed at all, and he
finished cooking something that went in the bin ten minutes later.

---

### 13. Refusing an item he cannot make

**Objective:** turn down an item the line cannot produce, and have the floor know
it was the kitchen's call.

A ticket comes in for the duck at 9:20, and there is one portion left that is
already spoken for by an order two minutes older. Tom rejects the item on the pass
tablet. It leaves his queue marked as his decision, not as a table changing its
mind, and Devin is told immediately so he can go back and offer the guest something
else while the rest of their order is still cooking. The distinction matters at the
end of the night, when the item is visible as a kitchen rejection rather than a
cancellation. On the old rail Tom would call it out and hope it landed, and often
what happened instead was that the plate simply never arrived: the server found out
when the rest of the table had finished eating.

## Eli Whitaker — new server, first shift

### 14. Taking his first order on his first night

**Objective:** get one table's order to the kitchen without help and without
embarrassing himself.

It is 6:40 on Eli's first shift and he has three tables. He has the printed menu in
his apron with his own notes on it, and he is holding the handheld in the other
hand. A guest asks for the salmon. Eli goes to add it and finds it is not there to
add — Tom pulled it an hour ago — so he says it has gone tonight and suggests
something else, which is the first thing all evening he has got right without
asking anyone. Later he mistypes a note and cannot see how to change it, so he
leaves it and mentions it to Devin at the pass. He gets through the shift using the
handheld for ordering and paper for everything he does not trust yet. The old setup
could not have caught the salmon for him; what it also could not do is teach him
anything, and **the product barely does either, since there is no training mode and
his first real use was a live table.**

## Priya Raman — guest

### 15. Reading the menu at the table

**Objective:** see what the restaurant offers, on her own phone, and then stop
thinking about it.

Priya's group sits down and there is one code on the table. She scans it, the menu
opens, and she reads it in the dim light without zooming while the others are still
settling in. The salmon is not listed, which she does not notice and never needed
to. Nothing asks her to install anything, hand over an email, or create an account,
and there is no button to order — so when she has decided, she tells Eli, who is
standing at her table with a handheld. The old single laminated menu passed between
four people worked, but slowly, and the QR menu she half expected would have been a
PDF she had to drag around the screen.
