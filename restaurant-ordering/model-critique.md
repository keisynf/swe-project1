# Model Critique: Restaurant Ordering and Status Platform

Claude's main strength was finding problems in its own work. For example, when I decided the kitchen would use a TV, it pointed out that a TV cannot accept the status changes the kitchen is responsible for. It also recorded unclear answers as open questions instead of guessing. Claude never lost a decision, which means that everything I decided during the conversation made it into the documents. It treated each answer as something to check against the rest of the product.

OpenAI did well in different areas. It applied a Moore-style statement in its first draft without being asked, while Claude only did so after I asked for it. It also generalized from a single example, rewriting the whole order-status table as precedence-ordered rules after I showed it one broken case. In addition, it wrote testable quality requirements from the first draft and kept its writing concise. OpenAI was strongest when the task had a clear structure to follow.

Both models had some of the same weaknesses. Both defaulted to UX-template personas with Goals and Needs, which Sommerville argues against, and neither looked at competitors until a prompt required a real alternative. Even then, Claude named specific products while OpenAI only named a generic POS suite. Both also let the vision grow into a specification. This suggests that both models follow common patterns by default, and that the book's guidance only shows up when the prompt asks for it directly.

Claude's own weaknesses were its length and its tendency to add things. Its longest vision was 3,289 words, and its first non-functional requirements included rules that could not be tested. When I resumed it for a final revision of the vision, it relied on its memory of the file instead of reading it, so its log described cutting a document that no longer existed. It also added details that were never decided, such as a monthly fee, and changed the status line without being asked.

In the case of OpenAI, its conversation was better than its documents. It proposed a `closed` state, an order-status rule, and who owns each transition in the chat, but left all three out of the revised vision until I asked for them. It also made surface-level fixes, such as renaming an existing story when asked for a new one, and declared open questions outside the release where Claude marked them "Undecided". Its first shortened vision followed every rule in the prompt but became so abstract that it no longer said what the product does. As a result, its output needed closer review, because its documents looked complete even when the substance was missing.

Some types of prompts worked better than others. Directive prompts that explained the reason behind each correction worked for both models. On the other hand, short open prompts such as "let's create the personas" produced template output from both models. The most reliable prompts were the format constraints taken from the book, such as the final vision prompt, which gave both models the same structure. In general, the more specific the prompt was about what a good result looks like, the less the two models differed.

Overall, Claude was the better product manager simulator. It caught contradictions in its own work, kept every decision, and flagged what was still undecided, and its weaknesses are cheap to fix. OpenAI was better at writing testable requirements and concise documents. However, it needed me to find the decisions it dropped and the rules that broke, which is the work a product manager is supposed to do.
