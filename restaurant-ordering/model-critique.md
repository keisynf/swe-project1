# Model Critique: Restaurant Ordering and Status Platform

Claude's main strength was that it was able to find problems in its own work. For example, when I decided the kitchen would use a TV, it pointed out that a TV cannot accept the status changes the kitchen is responsible for. It also did not guess when an answer was ambiguous; instead, it recorded unclear answers as open questions. Claude never lost a decision, which means that everything I decided during the conversation made it into the documents. This made it feel closer to a product manager than a writer, since it treated each answer as something to check against the rest of the product rather than something to add to the document.

OpenAI did well in different areas. It applied a Moore-style statement in its first draft without being asked, while Claude only did so after I asked for it. It was also able to generalize from a single example: when I gave it one broken case in the order-status table, it rewrote the whole table as precedence-ordered rules instead of patching that one case. In addition, it wrote testable quality requirements from the first draft and kept its writing concise. OpenAI was strongest when the task had a clear structure to follow, such as a template or a set of rules.

Both models had some of the same weaknesses. Both defaulted to UX-template personas with Goals and Needs, which Sommerville argues against, and neither looked at competitors without being asked. Both also let the vision grow into a specification, since they added each new decision to it. This suggests that both models follow common patterns by default, and that the book's guidance only shows up in their output when the prompt asks for it directly.

Claude's own weaknesses were its length and its loose quality requirements. Its longest vision was 3,289 words, and its first non-functional requirements mixed in design prescriptions and rules that could not be tested. Both problems come from the same tendency: Claude wrote down everything it thought was relevant, which helped it find gaps but made the documents harder to use as they were.

In the case of OpenAI, its conversation was better than its documents. It proposed a `closed` state, an order-status rule, and who owns each transition in the chat, but left all three out of the revised vision until I asked for them. It also tended to make surface-level fixes, such as renaming an existing story to satisfy a request for a new one. Lastly, where Claude marked unresolved items as "Undecided", OpenAI more often declared them outside the release without asking. Together, these mean that OpenAI's output needed closer review, because its documents looked complete even when decisions were missing.

Some types of prompts worked better than others. Directive prompts that explained the reason behind each correction worked for both models, and one concrete example worked better than asking a model to "find every mismatch". On the other hand, short open prompts such as "let's create the personas" produced template output from both models, which needed a round of corrections each time. The most reliable prompts were the format constraints taken from the book, which gave both models the same result. In general, the more specific the prompt was about what a good result looks like, the less the two models differed.

Overall, Claude was the better product manager simulator. It caught contradictions in its own work, kept every decision, and flagged what was still undecided instead of deciding it. Its weaknesses, length and loose non-functional requirements, are cheap to fix. OpenAI was better at writing testable requirements and concise documents. However, it needed me to find the decisions it dropped and the rules that broke, which is the work a product manager is supposed to do.
